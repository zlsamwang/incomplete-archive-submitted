# Artist's Statement (Draft — for revision)

*This is a first draft, meant as a starting point for your own voice and
argument, not a finished submission. Word count target: ~500 words.*

---

*The Incomplete Archive* began from a question I kept returning to while
reading Theresa Hak Kyung Cha's *Dictee*: what does it mean to reconstruct
a memory, or a history, from fragments that were never assembled to be read
together? Cha's text moves through interruption, translation, and gaps that
are never smoothed over for the reader's comfort. I didn't want to represent
that book, or borrow its history — the archive, the family, and the day in
question are entirely invented — but I wanted to build an interactive
structure that took its formal lesson seriously: that a fragmented
presentation can be more honest than a continuous one, and that some gaps
are not failures of the telling but part of what is actually true about the
record.

The player's own memory of that day is a single sentence — *she promised,
and she didn't come* — and the game's mechanical work is entirely about
what happens to a sentence like that once it's pressed against a paper
trail. Rather than a linear narrative, the player is given twenty-one
discrete records spread across four rooms, and the central mechanic is
*comparison*, not *collection*.
Finding an object does nothing on its own; a discovery only lands when the
player holds two or three items in mind at once — a sign-in time against a
duty log, a phone call against a transit notice, a child's unanswered
question against a reel that was never claimed. I tried to write the
evidence the way Cha's fragments function: complete in itself, but changing
meaning depending on what it sits beside. And I tried to be equally
disciplined about the reverse — building in real, load-bearing gaps (why
the relationship grew distant afterward; whether that distance connects to
this day at all) that the surviving materials cannot answer, and refusing
to let the final note resolve them anyway. The "reaches further" option at
the writing desk exists so that overreaching — deciding there's "nothing
left to forgive," for instance — is a real, felt choice the interface
doesn't quietly prevent, not an impossibility. The very last thing the
game offers — a short, optional replay that places a line from the
childhood recording next to a line from the mother's message, explicitly
labeled as something assembled *now* rather than a conversation that
happened *then* — is the most literal expression of this: it's the player
performing the same constructive act the whole game has asked of them,
made visible as a single, deliberate gesture.

The second reference, Walter Ong's *Orality and Literacy*, shaped the two
recordings directly. The player's own childhood recording ends on an
unanswered question — *can you play it for her?* — and the answer doesn't
exist anywhere in writing; it exists only on a second recording, made after
the fact, kept separately, and never played for anyone until now. Ong's
contrast between an utterance unfolding in time and a written record that
can be filed, cross-referenced, and read out of sequence is close to
literally what separates these two objects: the duty log can tell you
*that* your mother spoke to Mara and left something, but only the reel lets
you hear her say it, hesitations included. I wanted the interaction to make
a player feel that difference rather than be told about it — reading a
summary of a conversation is not the same act as sitting through its
pauses, even when a transcript is provided in both cases (as it always is
here, for accessibility). I was careful not to imply that the recording is
therefore more "true" than the logs that corroborate its timeline, or that
speech is inherently more honest than writing; the log and the reel agree
with each other, and both were necessary to trust either one. And because
the message is finished the moment the tape stops, but not lost — filed
correctly, waiting — I wanted the space between when a voice is recorded
and when it is finally heard to be the game's real subject, more than any
single fact inside it.

What I'm least sure I've solved: whether resolving the factual question so
early (she came; she was late; she explained some of it) leaves the
emotional question — what to do with that, now — room to feel genuinely
open, rather than like an epilogue tacked onto a mystery already solved. I
also gave the longer recording a segment index, so a player doesn't have
to sit through the whole session again to find the message — a concession
to pacing that sits in some tension with the Ong point about an utterance
unfolding in time rather than being indexed like a document. I don't think
that tension resolves cleanly; I made the practical choice and I'm noting
it rather than pretending it isn't there.
